Where DNA and RNA genuinely converge — the one hard problem

Recovery is easy for DNA. Telling near-identical copies apart is hard for both.

Finding that a copy exists is trivial for DNA and moderate for RNA. The real frontier — shared by both modalities — is resolving which of several ~99%-identical copies you are looking at.

DNA sequence alone

Push the identity floor down to catch divergent copies and it starts over-merging near-identical families — a sequence-only reconstruction lands at 81.5% recall precisely from these merges.

RNA transcripts alone

Near-identical copies emit near-identical reads (MAPQ-0, K=0) — no read-level signal to split them. This is the bulk of the resolution-limited misses.

Soto breaks the tie with parCN — paralog-specific copy number from WGS read depth. Rustle breaks it with read-level PSVs and copy-specific junctions. That is the axis on which RNA is not redundant with DNA — not recovery, but transcript-level resolution of the copies.

Bottom line

Through the same grouping engine, DNA (genome only) recovers 358/362 = 98.9% and RNA 313/362 = 86.5% — union 361/362. That is not RNA losing a recall contest: DNA answers “does the copy exist,” which the genome always knows, while RNA answers “is it expressed and resolvable,” which not every copy is.

The gap is 48 members DNA-only vs 3 RNA-only — a category difference, not error: silent copies plus copies too alike to separate, against a handful of divergent copies only RNA reaches. And on the one problem genuinely hard for both, DNA leans on copy number (parCN) and RNA on read-level variants. That resolution axis is where the transcriptome earns its place next to the genome.
